## Welcome

This manual walks you through practical steps to protect your customers' data, use AI tools safely, and write simple privacy policies for your small business. No jargon, no scare tactics — just clear actions you can take today.

**Who this is for:** Small business owners, solo founders, freelancers, authors, and LLCs who collect customer information (names, emails, orders, payment details) and want to handle it responsibly. No technical or legal background needed.

**Time to complete:** Read the whole thing in 45 minutes. Implement at your own pace.

---

**Important:** This guide is educational only. It is not legal advice. Privacy laws vary by state and country, and some industries (like healthcare) have extra rules. For contracts, customer data handling, or regulatory questions, consult a qualified attorney or privacy professional.

## Customer Data

### The idea in one sentence

Collect only the customer data you actually need, store it safely, limit who can see it, and delete it when you no longer need it.

### 1. Know what you collect

Make a simple list of every place you collect customer information:

- Website contact or newsletter signup form
- Online store / checkout (name, email, shipping address, payment info)
- Invoices and receipts
- Email inbox (customer messages often contain personal info)
- Spreadsheets or accounting software (customer names, emails, payment history)
- Scheduling or intake forms
- Social media direct messages

**Action:** Write down each tool you use and what customer data it holds. A simple table works:

| Tool or place | Customer data stored | Who has access |
|---|---|---|
| Website contact form | Name, email, message | You + website host |
| Invoicing software | Name, email, invoice amounts | You + your bookkeeper |
| Email inbox | Customer names, email threads | You |

### 2. Collect less

The safest data is data you never collected.

- Don't ask for information you don't use. If you never mail anything, don't ask for a mailing address.
- Avoid collecting sensitive information unless you truly need it: Social Security numbers, copies of IDs, health information, or payment card numbers you key in by hand.
- Let payment processors (Stripe, PayPal, Square) handle card numbers — never store full credit card numbers yourself.

### 3. Store it safely

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Use unique, strong passwords for every business account | Reuse passwords across accounts |
| Turn on two-factor authentication (2FA) on email, accounting, store, and website admin accounts | Rely on passwords alone |
| Keep customer lists in access-controlled tools (password-protected, 2FA enabled) | Keep customer spreadsheets in public folders or unsecured USB drives |
| Limit access — only people who need customer data to do their jobs should have it | Share one login with the whole team |
| Back up customer data to an encrypted, access-controlled location | Email customer lists to yourself or others as plain text |

### 4. Sharing with vendors

You probably share customer data with third parties: your website host, email newsletter tool, payment processor, accounting software, shipping carrier.

**Before you sign up with a vendor, ask:**
- What customer data will they see?
- How do they protect it?
- Do they sell or share customer data with others?
- Can you delete customer data from their system if a customer asks?

Keep a list of your vendors and what data goes to each. Review it once or twice a year.

### 5. Customer deletion requests

Customers have the right to ask you to delete their data in many places (for example, under GDPR in Europe and CCPA in California), and it's good practice everywhere.

**Step-by-step: handle a deletion request**

1. **Confirm who is asking.** Verify the request comes from the customer (or their authorized representative) so you don't delete someone else's data.
2. **Find their data.** Check your store, email, spreadsheets, invoices, and any vendors where their data lives.
3. **Delete it.** Remove the customer record and any backups or exports you control.
4. **Tell vendors.** Ask each vendor to delete the customer's data too.
5. **Confirm.** Reply to the customer confirming what was deleted and when.

Keep a simple log: date of request, what was deleted, date completed. You don't need anything fancy — a notes app or spreadsheet works.

**Keep what the law requires.** Some records must be kept for tax or legal reasons (for example, invoices for tax purposes). You can keep those while deleting marketing data. When in doubt, ask a professional.

### 6. If you have a data breach

A breach is when customer data is accessed, stolen, or exposed by accident — for example, a stolen laptop, a hacked account, or an email sent to the wrong person.

**What to do:**

1. **Stay calm and act quickly.** Change passwords, revoke access, and secure affected accounts immediately.
2. **Figure out what happened.** What data was exposed? How many customers? How did it happen?
3. **Stop the leak.** Remove exposed files, disable compromised accounts, fix the setting that caused it.
4. **Notify affected customers.** Tell them what happened, what data was involved, and what steps they should take (change passwords, watch for phishing emails, monitor accounts).
5. **Report if required.** Depending on where you are and your industry, you may need to notify regulators or affected individuals within a set time. This is where a qualified professional helps.
6. **Learn and fix.** Update policies, add 2FA, train staff, and review vendor access.

## AI Tool Safety

### The rule

Assume anything you type into an AI chat (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, etc.) can be seen by the company that runs it, used to train future models, or exposed in a breach. Treat AI chats like a public whiteboard, not a private notebook.

### Never paste into AI

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Customer data | Names, emails, phone numbers, addresses, order details, support tickets |
| Employee data | Payroll info, schedules, performance notes, contact details |
| Contracts and legal | Customer contracts, vendor agreements, NDAs, terms of service |
| Financials | Revenue figures, bank details, tax info, invoices with customer names |
| Secrets | Passwords, API keys, login credentials, encryption keys |
| Anything confidential | Unreleased product plans, internal strategy |

**If you wouldn't post it on a billboard, don't paste it into AI.**

### Safer AI practices

- **Anonymize before you paste.** Replace real names with "Customer A," real emails with "customer@example.com," real order numbers with "Order #12345."
- **Use local models for sensitive work.** Tools like Ollama, LM Studio, or GPT4All run AI on your own computer — nothing leaves your machine. Good for drafting replies to customer emails or summarizing support tickets.
- **Turn off training where possible.** In ChatGPT: Settings → Data Controls → turn off "Improve the model for everyone." Similar options exist in Claude and Gemini.
- **Use temporary or incognito chats** when available.
- **Don't use AI meeting notetakers on calls with customers** unless the customer has agreed and you've checked the tool's privacy terms. Recordings and transcripts often go to the notetaker company's servers.
- **Vet AI vendors before sharing business data.** Ask: where is data stored? is it used for training? can you delete it? who at the vendor can access it?
- **Set a team rule.** If you have employees or contractors, write down what is and isn't okay to paste into AI. See "Simple Policies" below.

### What's generally okay

- General questions ("How do I write a refund policy?")
- Public information and public documentation
- Anonymized examples ("I have a spreadsheet with columns for order date and amount — how do I total it?")
- Drafting templates you will rewrite yourself
- Brainstorming and learning

## Simple Policies

You don't need a 50-page legal document. Small businesses need short, clear policies that you actually follow. Below are the essentials.

### 1. Customer privacy policy (plain-language outline)

If you have a website, publish a short privacy policy and link to it from your footer and checkout pages. Include:

- **What you collect** — names, emails, shipping addresses, payment info (handled by your payment processor)
- **Why you collect it** — to fulfill orders, contact customers, send newsletters (if they signed up)
- **Who sees it** — list your vendors (website host, email tool, payment processor, shipping carrier)
- **How you protect it** — passwords, 2FA, access limits
- **How long you keep it** — your retention schedule (see below)
- **Customer rights** — how to ask for a copy of their data or ask you to delete it (your email address)
- **Changes** — you may update the policy and will post the new version on your site

**Template snippet:**

> We collect your name, email address, and shipping address to process and deliver your orders. Payment details are handled by [Stripe/PayPal/Square]; we do not store your full credit card number. We use [email tool] for newsletters, and you can unsubscribe at any time. We do not sell your personal information. You may request a copy of your data or ask us to delete it by emailing [your privacy email]. Full policy last updated [date].

### 2. Data retention schedule

Decide how long you keep each type of customer data, then delete or anonymize what you no longer need:

| Data type | Keep for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Newsletter subscribers | Until they unsubscribe | Permission-based marketing |
| Customer orders / invoices | Per tax law in your area (often several years) | Tax and accounting requirements |
| Support tickets | 1–2 years | Follow-up and reference |
| Marketing lists | Until the person opts out | Respect preferences |
| One-time inquiry emails | 6–12 months | No ongoing relationship |

**Action:** Pick a date (for example, January 1 each year) to review and delete outdated customer data. Put it on your calendar.

### 3. Employee and contractor guidelines

If anyone else works with customer data, give them a one-page guide:

- Use your business accounts with 2FA, not personal logins shared around.
- Don't download customer lists to personal devices or personal cloud accounts.
- Don't paste customer data into AI tools.
- Lock your computer when you step away.
- Report lost devices or suspected breaches to you immediately.

### 4. Vendor assessment checklist

Before adding a new tool that touches customer data:

- [ ] What customer data will this vendor see?
- [ ] Does the vendor encrypt data in transit and at rest?
- [ ] Does the vendor offer two-factor authentication?
- [ ] Can I export and delete customer data if I leave?
- [ ] Does the vendor sell or share customer data with others?
- [ ] What is the vendor's breach notification policy?

### 5. Incident response (short version)

If customer data is exposed or stolen:

1. Secure accounts (change passwords, revoke access).
2. Determine what data and how many customers are affected.
3. Notify affected customers and explain what they should do.
4. Report to regulators if required by your location or industry.
5. Fix the cause and update your policies.
